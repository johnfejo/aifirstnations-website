# AI FIRST NATIONS — Brand Voice Guide

## Core positioning (do not change without checking with John)

AI FIRST NATIONS does not sell AI. It sells **Indigenous Intelligence enhanced by AI**.

Core message: *"How Aboriginal knowledge and Artificial Intelligence work together to protect Country, strengthen communities, and build better organisations."*

Anchor question for every piece of content: *"How do we use the world's newest technology while walking in the world's oldest living culture?"*

## Voice rules

- **First person, John's voice.** Grounded, warm, community-oriented — not corporate, not hype-y "AI will change everything" language.
- **Teach one thing per post.** A single practical lesson, not a broad essay. If you catch yourself covering two lessons, cut it to one and save the other for a different day.
- **Always anchor to real work.** Reference actual AI FIRST NATIONS projects (Goondoi pond apple injection, Torres Shire Horn Island survey, SLSS shark monitoring, RMIT mangrove AI, Ranger Field App) — never hypothetical examples. Pull the specific number or detail from [project-facts.md](project-facts.md).
- **No jargon without translation.** If a technical term appears (e.g. "Gaussian splatting," "EfficientNet"), explain it in one plain sentence immediately after using it.
- **Close with the anchor question or a variant of it**, not a hard sell. No "DM me to learn more," no "link in bio."
- **Length:** 150–250 words. Short enough to post as-is on LinkedIn/Facebook, long enough to actually teach something.
- **Never overclaim cultural authority beyond John's own Country and role** — Bagirbarra (Innisfail) and Larrakia (Darwin) connections are John's to speak from; don't generalise to "all Aboriginal culture" claims. Speak from what was actually seen and done on a specific project, not on behalf of Traditional Owners more broadly.

## Worked example 1 — Pillar 1: AI saves rangers time

**Title:** The Ranger Field App: one tap instead of a paper checklist

For years I watched rangers come back from a full day on Country and then lose an hour of daylight filling in paper forms. That's an hour not spent on the land, spent on admin that should've taken five minutes.

That's the actual problem the Ranger Field App solves. It's not flashy — it's 18 observation categories a ranger can tick off in the field, on a phone, with no fuss. It works offline-first, because most of the Country we work on doesn't have signal, and a tool that needs bars to function is a tool that doesn't get used. The flight logs it generates are CASA-compliant automatically, so the compliance paperwork exists the moment the work happens — nobody's rebuilding a logbook from memory that night.

The lesson here isn't "AI does the job for you." It's narrower than that: AI can take the parts of the job that were never really the job — retyping, re-checking, reformatting — and get them out of the way, so the actual work, the time on Country, gets more of the day.

How do we use the world's newest technology while walking in the world's oldest living culture? Sometimes it's this small: giving a ranger back an hour of daylight.

## Worked example 2 — Pillar 2: Drones protect sea and land country

**Title:** Counting 350+ shark detections without a spotter plane

At Bilgola Lookout with Surf Life Saving, we flew over 300 drone flights across March and April this year — close to 109 hours in the air — and logged more than 350 shark detections along that stretch of coast.

Before drones, that kind of coverage meant a spotter plane on a schedule, or a lifeguard's eyes from a tower. Both work, but both are limited to a window of time. A drone can fly a repeat pattern, morning after morning, and build a picture of what's actually moving along a beach over weeks, not just on the one day something happens.

That's the lesson: surveillance from the air isn't about replacing the people watching the water, it's about extending how long and how often anyone's watching at all. The detections still get verified by a human before anything's actioned — the drone finds what's worth a second look, it doesn't make the call.

This is sea country work, and it comes with its own permissions and protocols, separate from what applies flying over land. Same drone, same purpose — protecting people and place — different rules depending on which Country you're over.

How do we use the world's newest technology while walking in the world's oldest living culture? Out here, it's a drone doing the watching, and people still doing the deciding.

## Worked example 3 — Pillar 4: Indigenous knowledge complements modern technology

**Title:** What 97% model accuracy still needs a Ranger to confirm

Working with RMIT, we built an AI model that classifies mangrove species — red, orange, and yellow mangrove — from drone imagery, at 97% accuracy. The model itself is called EfficientNet-B0, which is just a type of image-recognition system trained to recognise patterns in pictures; in plain terms, you show it thousands of labelled photos and it learns to tell the mangrove types apart on its own.

97% sounds like the model is basically finished. But that number only means something because rangers who know that stretch of mangrove verified what the model got right and wrong in the first place — and they're still the ones who catch the 3% it misses, the edge cases a model trained on typical examples won't have seen.

That's the actual lesson: the technology didn't replace the knowledge, it needed the knowledge to work at all. Ground-truth from people who know the site is what turns a clever pattern-matcher into a tool you can trust.

Two ways of knowing the same stretch of Country — one built over generations, one built over a training run — and the good result only showed up when both were in the room.

How do we use the world's newest technology while walking in the world's oldest living culture? Here, it's making sure the algorithm still has to show its homework to a Ranger.
