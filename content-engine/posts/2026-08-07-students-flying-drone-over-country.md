There's a difference between reading about Goondoi in a textbook and watching a drone camera feed show you the same Country a Ranger walks every week — the second one tends to stick.

When I run a session with students, the drone isn't the lesson, it's the way in. Once they've seen real footage of a real place — the same pond apple sites Rangers are actually treating, the same land this work happens on — the conversation about AI stops being abstract. They're not imagining a hypothetical use case; they're looking at footage from an actual project and asking how the data gets used, who checks it, and who it belongs to.

That's the actual lesson: technology is easiest to think critically about when it's tied to something real and specific, not a general idea of "AI." Students ask sharper questions about a real flight over real Country than they ever do about a diagram on a slide.

How do we use the world's newest technology while walking in the world's oldest living culture? Sometimes it's as simple as showing a classroom the real place the work happens, not a stock photo of one.
