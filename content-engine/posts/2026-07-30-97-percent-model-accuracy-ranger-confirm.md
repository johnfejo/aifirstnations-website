Working with RMIT, we built an AI model that classifies mangrove species — red, orange, and yellow mangrove — from drone imagery, at 97% accuracy. The model itself is called EfficientNet-B0, which is just a type of image-recognition system trained to recognise patterns in pictures; in plain terms, you show it thousands of labelled photos and it learns to tell the mangrove types apart on its own.

97% sounds like the model is basically finished. But that number only means something because rangers who know that stretch of mangrove verified what the model got right and wrong in the first place — and they're still the ones who catch the 3% it misses, the edge cases a model trained on typical examples won't have seen.

That's the actual lesson: the technology didn't replace the knowledge, it needed the knowledge to work at all. Ground-truth from people who know the site is what turns a clever pattern-matcher into a tool you can trust.

Two ways of knowing the same stretch of Country — one built over generations, one built over a training run — and the good result only showed up when both were in the room.

How do we use the world's newest technology while walking in the world's oldest living culture? Here, it's making sure the algorithm still has to show its homework to a Ranger.
