Before any herbicide goes into a tree at Goondoi, a mapping flight goes up first. That order matters more than it sounds like it should.

Pond apple doesn't grow in tidy rows — it's scattered through dense vegetation, and walking a site to count every stem by eye means missing some and wasting time on ground that's already been checked. A drone survey over the site turns that guesswork into an actual count and location for every tree that needs treating, so a session — 500-plus trees, treated by stem injection with Glyphosate 360 g/L — goes in with a plan instead of a search.

That's the practical value: not the flight itself, but what it prevents. Fewer missed trees. No re-walking ground twice. No more herbicide used than the plan actually calls for, because the plan is based on what's really out there, not an estimate.

Drones get talked about for the dramatic footage. The genuinely useful part, most days, is this — a map that turns a full day of searching into a morning of treating.

How do we use the world's newest technology while walking in the world's oldest living culture? Here, it's mapping first so the actual work on Country goes further.
