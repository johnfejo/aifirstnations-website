Over March and April this year, we flew more than 300 shark-monitoring flights at Bilgola Lookout — close to 109 hours in the air, across two months.

Spread that out and it's not a dramatic number on any single day. A handful of flights, a bit over an hour of flying, most mornings. But that's exactly the point: what makes aerial monitoring useful isn't one long flight, it's consistency — the same stretch of coast checked morning after morning, so a pattern can actually show up in the data instead of a single snapshot.

Over those two months, that steady coverage logged more than 350 shark detections. None of them would have shown up from a single flight on a single good-weather day. They showed up because the same beach got watched, repeatedly, for long enough that something real could be seen in it.

The lesson isn't about the technology being impressive — it's about what repetition buys you that a one-off never can.

How do we use the world's newest technology while walking in the world's oldest living culture? Out here, it's showing up morning after morning, same as anyone who actually knows a piece of coast.
