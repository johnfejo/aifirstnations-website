A lot of contracts I've seen have an Indigenous engagement clause sitting in the boilerplate — a paragraph that gets signed once and then never referred to again for the life of the project.

On the Torres Shire Horn Island survey, engagement wasn't a clause you'd sign and file away — it was an operating rhythm. Which of the four sites got flown, and in what order across the eleven routes, stayed open to input from Traditional Owners through the life of the survey, not just at the point the contract was executed. That meant check-ins during the work, not just before it.

A clause on a page can't do that on its own. It can set an intention, but only ongoing contact during delivery actually tests whether that intention holds up when a schedule gets tight or a site needs reconsidering.

If a business partner asks me what makes an engagement genuine, that's the answer: does it survive contact with the actual timeline, or does it only exist on the page it was signed on?

How do we use the world's newest technology while walking in the world's oldest living culture? A signature is where an engagement starts — not where it ends.
