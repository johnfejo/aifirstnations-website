Before a drone maps a pond apple infestation at Goondoi, the Rangers already have a rough idea where it'll be — Country holds the same patterns year to year, and people who've worked that land for a long time read it before any flight goes up.

What the drone adds isn't a replacement for that knowledge, it's a second, independent check on it. A mapping flight over the site turns what was known in general terms — "it's thick along that gully" — into an exact count and location, useful for planning exactly how many trees a treatment session needs to cover. Over 500 trees a session, treated by stem injection, based on a map that confirmed what the Rangers expected and caught the parts they couldn't see from the ground.

Neither version of Country-knowledge stands alone here. The old way narrows down where to look. The new tool confirms exactly what's there. Treat either one as sufficient by itself and you'd get a worse result than using both.

How do we use the world's newest technology while walking in the world's oldest living culture? At Goondoi, it's reading the land first, then letting a drone check the reading.
