On the Goondoi project, the survey boundaries and the treatment method weren't decided by a client brief — they were set by the Traditional Owners, before a single flight plan existed.

That order matters more than it sounds like it should. When governance happens at the end, it's a sign-off — a box ticked on a plan someone else already wrote. When it happens first, it actually shapes what the technology is asked to do. At Goondoi, that meant the pond apple treatment used stem injection — a method where herbicide is injected directly into the trunk rather than sprayed — because it was agreed as the least disruptive option for that particular stretch of Country. Over 500 trees get treated a session this way. The method wasn't a technical default; it came out of the governance conversation, not around it.

The lesson isn't "consult Traditional Owners." It's narrower: the earlier that conversation happens, the more it actually changes — the scope, the method, sometimes the whole approach — rather than just approving a plan that was already fixed.

How do we use the world's newest technology while walking in the world's oldest living culture? At Goondoi, it started with a conversation, not a drone.
