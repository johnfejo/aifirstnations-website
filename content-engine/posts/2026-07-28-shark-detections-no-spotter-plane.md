At Bilgola Lookout with Surf Life Saving, we flew over 300 drone flights across March and April this year — close to 109 hours in the air — and logged more than 350 shark detections along that stretch of coast.

Before drones, that kind of coverage meant a spotter plane on a schedule, or a lifeguard's eyes from a tower. Both work, but both are limited to a window of time. A drone can fly a repeat pattern, morning after morning, and build a picture of what's actually moving along a beach over weeks, not just on the one day something happens.

That's the lesson: surveillance from the air isn't about replacing the people watching the water, it's about extending how long and how often anyone's watching at all. The detections still get verified by a human before anything's actioned — the drone finds what's worth a second look, it doesn't make the call.

This is sea country work, and it comes with its own permissions and protocols, separate from what applies flying over land. Same drone, same purpose — protecting people and place — different rules depending on which Country you're over.

How do we use the world's newest technology while walking in the world's oldest living culture? Out here, it's a drone doing the watching, and people still doing the deciding.
