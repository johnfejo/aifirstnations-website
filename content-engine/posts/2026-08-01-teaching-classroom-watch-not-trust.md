I don't start school sessions with a slide about neural networks. I start with a number: 350. That's how many shark detections a drone model flagged over Bilgola Lookout this year, across 300-plus flights. Then I ask the class: how many of those would you trust without a person checking first?

That's the actual lesson I want students to walk away with. AI isn't magic and it isn't infallible — it's a tool that flags what's worth a second look, and a person still makes the call. A detection model, in plain terms, is just software trained to spot a pattern in an image, the same way a person learns to recognise a shape after seeing it enough times. It gets it right a lot. It doesn't get it right every time.

Students remember that idea a lot longer when it's tied to a real flight over a real beach than when it's an abstract diagram. And teaching it through an Indigenous lens isn't a side note — it's asking the same question I ask on every project: whose knowledge checks the machine's answer, and who's accountable for what happens next.

How do we use the world's newest technology while walking in the world's oldest living culture? In a classroom, it starts with teaching kids to ask that question themselves.
